    Underground Pipe Damage: Common Causes and Modern Repair Solutions

    By Luis Ortega · August 31, 2026 · 5 min read

    What Goes Wrong Under the Yard

    Your sewer lateral and water service run from the house to the street, usually somewhere 2 to 6 feet underground. Nobody thinks about them until a toilet backs up or the lawn turns soggy. A typical lateral is 4-inch pipe, and older homes often have clay tile or cast iron. Each fails in its own way.

    Common Causes

    Tree Roots

    Roots find sewer lines by following moisture from a cracked joint or a loose gasket. A single fine root gets inside, then spreads into a mat that snags paper and grease. Clay pipe is the worst for this. The joints every two or three feet give roots a way in, and a mature oak or maple can fill a 4-inch line in a few seasons.

    Shifting Soil

    Soils in Maryland range from heavy clay in the central counties to sandy, low-lying ground near the shore, and each affects buried lines differently.

    Soil that settles or swells moves the pipe with it. Rigid pipe like clay or cast iron does not bend, so it either cracks or separates at a joint. Sags are common too. A sag, which we call a belly, holds water and waste, and on camera it looks like a stretch that stays full after flushing.

    Old Pipe Material

    Homes built after the war usually have copper supply lines, while newer construction relies on PEX and plastic drain piping.

    Age wears every material down. Cast iron rusts from the inside, and the bottom of the pipe can corrode through while the top still looks solid. Orangeburg pipe, used through the early 1970s, is pressed tar paper that slowly flattens into an oval. Old galvanized water lines rust shut inside and drop the pressure everywhere.

    Signs of a Buried Line Problem

    Several drains slowing at once points to the main line. One classic sign is a toilet that gurgles as the washer empties. In the yard, watch for a strip of grass that is greener than the rest, or ground that feels soft over the line during dry weather. Sewer smell near the cleanout is another.

    On the water side, the meter tells the story. Turn off every fixture and watch the leak indicator, the small triangle or dial on the meter face. When that indicator spins, water is escaping between the meter and the house, often from the buried service. Low pressure throughout the house can mean the same.

    A sewer camera settles it. We run a camera through the cleanout and record the whole line, with a locator that marks depth and position on the ground above. We show you the footage as it plays. The footage decides the repair.

    Modern Repair Options

    Trench repair is still the right call for some jobs. A collapsed section or a line with a deep belly usually has to be dug up, since lining needs a pipe that still holds its shape. Spot repairs can be small. One cracked joint might only need a four-by-six hole and a day on site.

    Trenchless lining avoids most of the digging. We pull a resin-soaked felt liner into the old pipe, inflate it, and let it cure into a new pipe inside the old one. Most residential liners go in through the cleanout and one small access pit, and the job takes a day. Roots cannot get through it.

    Pipe bursting is the other trenchless method. A cone-shaped head is winched through the old pipe, splitting it and pulling new HDPE pipe into place behind it. You need a pit on each end, but the lawn in between stays untouched. Our crews at BVL Plumbing use both methods across Maryland, depending on what the camera shows.

    When to Call

    Do not wait on a sewer line that has backed up twice. Every backup leaves more waste behind, and that feeds the roots. Most of our camera inspections take under an hour, and you get a copy of the video plus a marked-up location of the problem. Reach BVL Plumbing at (443) 719-1650. It helps if you know where your cleanout is, because that saves time on the visit.

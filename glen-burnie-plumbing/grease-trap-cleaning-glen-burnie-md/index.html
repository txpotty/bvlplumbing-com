    Frequently Asked Questions About Grease Trap Cleaning in Glen Burnie

    Small under-sink traps often start around $200 a visit. Large outdoor interceptors can pass $1,000 when they are full and the cap has hardened. The biggest variable is the gallons hauled, because disposal facilities charge by volume and a 1,500-gallon tank is a full truckload.

    The terms get mixed up constantly. Most people use trap for the small indoor unit, usually under 100 gallons. Interceptor usually means the large tank in the ground outside, often 500 gallons and up, and codes size the two in different ways.

    Under-sink hydromechanical traps and in-ground gravity interceptors make up most of our work. Automatic grease removal units are on the list too. An AGRU uses a heater and skimmer wheel to move grease into a bin, and the bin, wheel and strainer all need regular cleaning.

    Watch the floor sinks during dish rush. Standing water in a floor sink is the first hint. Other signs include a rancid smell near the dish station, grease showing up in the cleanout nearest the street, and drains that gurgle after the dish machine empties.

    Rules come from your sewer district or city pretreatment program. Most follow some version of the 25 percent rule. Many also set a maximum interval, require records on site for a year or more, and ban enzyme additives as a substitute for pumping, so we check the program that covers your address before setting a schedule.

    Zurn, Schier, Watts and Big Dipper units turn up across Maryland every week, along with Rockford traps and older steel boxes from makers that are long gone. We can usually find parts. On discontinued models, a universal baffle or flow control often fits, and if the steel has rusted through we quote a replacement.

    There is no parts warranty on a pump-out, since nothing gets installed. The work itself is guaranteed. Should it drain poorly after our visit, or a backup trace back to something we missed, the crew returns and makes it right.

    Everything in the trap comes out, from the floating grease cap down to the sludge of food solids on the floor. The industry calls it brown grease. Yellow grease, the used fryer oil in your collection bin, is a separate product that gets recycled and should never go into the trap.

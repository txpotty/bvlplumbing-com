    Frequently Asked Questions About Grease Trap Installation in Severn

    Installed prices for small indoor hydromechanical traps typically fall between $1,500 and $5,000. Outdoor tanks cost more, mostly for digging and putting the site back. Brand is a small part of it. We give you a written estimate after we walk the site, and it does not change unless we hit something underground nobody could see.

    Watch the pump-out bills. A trap that needs service far more often than it used to is either too small or broken inside. Smell is another clue. Slow floor sinks, grease odor at the trap, a lid that no longer seals, or a failed FOG inspection all point the same way, and we can tell you which it is in one visit.

    For indoor traps we lean on Zurn, Watts, Schier and Endura, because their parts are easy to find and their units carry PDI or ASME certification. We like the plastic bodies from Schier where steel would rust out. Brand matters less than sizing. We pick the rating first, then the brand that fits the space and budget.

    Size comes from flow, and flow comes from your fixtures. We add up the drain rate of each sink compartment, floor sink and dish machine, then apply the code's drain-down time to get gallons per minute. Gravity tanks use volume instead. Outdoor tanks get sized by the sewer authority's formula, which we run for you.

    Two warranties apply here. The trap body carries the maker's coverage, which runs from one year on some steel units to much longer on plastic. Our labor warranty covers the joints and the setting, and you get it in writing up front.

    Plan on a permit. Grease interceptors are regulated by both the plumbing code and the local sewer use ordinance, so two offices may want to see the plans. You get copies of every approval for your files. Unpermitted traps cause trouble at lease transfer.

    Coverage comes from two places. The manufacturer warrants the trap body and lid, and the term depends on the brand and whether it is steel or plastic. Our written labor warranty covers the joints we made and how we set the unit. Damage from skipped cleanings falls outside it.

    Plastic hydromechanical traps often last 20 years or more, while coated steel traps can rust out in 7 to 15 depending on how harsh the wastewater runs. A precast interceptor can last a very long time if it is pumped on schedule and the coating holds. Cleaning habits matter most. A trap that is scraped with a metal tool every week or left full of grease for months ages much faster than one that gets regular service.

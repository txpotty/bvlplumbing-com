    Frequently Asked Questions About New Construction Plumbing in Waldorf

    Bathroom count drives the cost more than square footage does. A square-foot guess misses too much, so we bid from plans.

    Most jurisdictions inspect plumbing three times. First comes underground, with drains holding a water or air test before the pour. Top-out gets inspected before insulation, and the final comes after fixtures are set and running. Some add a gas test.

    For supply, we use PEX-A or PEX-B in most homes, sometimes with a copper main. Drain, waste and vent runs are PVC or ABS. Cast iron is still used in some multifamily buildings and in walls next to bedrooms where drain noise matters, since it is much quieter than plastic when a toilet flushes upstairs.

    Scheduling causes most of the trouble. A late pour or a framing change can stack plumbing phases on top of each other. Late plan changes are next, since moving a tub after framing means more drilling and another inspection. Every change gets priced in writing before we move pipe.

    Slow drains in a brand-new house are the first sign. Gurgling fixtures or sewer odor point to venting or slope problems. A camera inspection through the cleanout will show a belly, an offset joint or debris left in the line, and it is worth doing before the builder's warranty period ends. Construction debris is common.

    We can run a dedicated return line during rough-in. Hot water then reaches distant bathrooms in seconds. A dedicated line works better than a crossover valve under a sink, which sends hot water back through the cold line and can leave the cold tap lukewarm for a moment. The pump needs an outlet.

    We do, mostly on custom homes. We work from your architect's plans and suggest changes that shorten runs or improve flow. Stacking baths, moving the water heater closer to the main bath and adding a recirculation line are the usual ones. Small moves save pipe.
